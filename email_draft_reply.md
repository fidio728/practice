# Draft Email Reply to Emanuele

Hi Emanuele,

Thank you for the four papers — they were very helpful in sharpening the idea. Here is where things stand after a week of work.

**Positioning relative to the literature.** My idea differs from the EDGAR-based papers (Gao and Huang 2020; Loughran and McDonald 2017) in one key respect: EDGAR primarily hosts financial disclosures — 10-Ks, 10-Qs, 8-Ks — whereas corporate websites contain a much richer set of voluntary information: multi-hundred-page strategy documents, ESG reports, product roadmaps, and real-time press releases that are far more timely than annual filings. The idea is closest to Cao, Jiang, Yang, and Zhang (2023), who study how firms adapt *what they say* when machines are listening. I want to study whether firms allow AI to *read at all*, and if so, *what* they allow AI to read. Importantly, the GenAI search bots in my setting (OAI-SearchBot, Claude-SearchBot) are fundamentally different from the NLP parsers in their paper — these bots fetch and synthesize web content in real time for end users, which means blocking them has a direct and immediate effect on how investors receive information.

**A problem I ran into.** Last week I pushed the idea toward information processing costs: if a firm blocks AI search bots, GenAI tools cannot retrieve the firm's corporate website, which should raise the cost of acquiring information about that firm. But I realized this logic has a serious weakness. Even if a firm blocks AI search bots on its corporate website, GenAI tools will simply query alternative sources — EDGAR for financial questions, Bloomberg or Reuters for earnings, news outlets and podcasts for strategy updates. For well-covered firms, the marginal information processing cost of a corporate website block may be close to zero, because substitutes are abundant. This led me to ask a more fundamental question: what exactly are firms blocking, and does it matter?

**What I found.** I ran a systematic scan of robots.txt files across 55 publicly traded U.S. firms, stratified by market capitalization (mega, large, mid, small cap). The critical finding is that firms do not simply "block AI" or "allow AI" — they make granular choices about *what content* to protect and *what type of AI access* to permit.

On the content dimension, firms consistently block AI from their commercially valuable content while keeping everything else open. Amazon blocks all AI bots on amazon.com (the retail site — protecting product data, pricing, and reviews), but imposes zero AI restrictions on aboutamazon.com (the corporate news and investor relations site). Goldman Sachs blocks GPTBot specifically on /research/ and /insights/ — its paid analyst content — while keeping the rest of goldmansachs.com open. Shutterstock, an IP-intensive content business, blocks both search and training bots across its entire site. The common logic is simple: firms block AI from whatever generates revenue. Amazon's retail data, Goldman's research, and Shutterstock's images are all core commercial assets that lose value if AI can freely ingest and redistribute them. Meanwhile, corporate news, press releases, and investor relations pages are kept open — because firms *want* that information to reach investors, including through AI-powered search.

On the access-type dimension, AI crawlers come in two types: training bots (GPTBot, ClaudeBot) that collect data to build model weights, and search bots (OAI-SearchBot, Claude-SearchBot) that fetch content in real time when a user asks a question. Some firms treat these differently. Meta, for instance, blocks training bots on about.meta.com but explicitly allows search bots. Blocking a training bot means the firm's content will not be permanently absorbed into a competitor's AI model — it is a form of IP protection. Allowing a search bot means the firm's information can still be surfaced to users who ask about it — it preserves visibility. The question is what predicts this choice across firms, and whether the distinction between training access and search access has measurable consequences for information production or market outcomes.

Across the 50 firms with readable robots.txt files on their corporate domains, only 2% root-block AI search bots and only 4% root-block AI training bots. No firm blocks Googlebot on its corporate domain. The granular patterns I described above — content-selective blocking and the training/search distinction — are real, but the sample sizes are very small: only one firm (Meta) differentiates between training and search bots, and only a handful selectively block specific paths. This is not enough variation to support a large-sample empirical study in the current form.

I think this is a genuinely interesting setting, but I may be looking at it from the wrong angle. I would appreciate your perspective on whether there is a viable research design here, or whether it would be better to pivot. Happy to meet whenever convenient.
